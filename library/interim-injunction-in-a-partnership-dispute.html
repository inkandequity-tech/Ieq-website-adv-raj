Interim Injunction in a Partnership Dispute

How can a party get an interim injunction to preserve property during a civil dispute?

Illustrative scenario. This is a hypothetical illustrative scenario prepared for general legal information. It does not describe an actual client matter or guarantee any particular outcome. The applicable law and available remedies depend on the facts and jurisdiction; this page is not legal advice.

IHypothetical facts

Two fictional partners run a trading firm from premises registered in one partner's name, which the parties claim were acquired for or used by the firm. The title documents, source of purchase money, partnership deed and applicable property law do not conclusively establish firm ownership on these facts alone. After a breakdown, one partner learns the other has agreed to sell the premises to an outsider. The accounts of the firm are unsettled and no dissolution has been agreed.

IIThe practical problem

If the premises are sold, the firm's base, a major asset and the possible basis of a settlement could disappear before the dispute is decided.

IIIIssues

  1. Is there an arguable right in the premises or in the firm's accounts that merits protection?
  2. Prima facie case, balance of convenience, irreparable injury.
  3. Candour, delay and clean hands.
  4. Is an injunction the right remedy, or is compensation adequate?
  5. What happens to a purchaser who buys during the suit?

IVLegal framework

  • Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, Order XXXIX Rules 1 and 2 (temporary injunctions), Rule 3 (notice and the exceptional ex parte grant, with the requirement to record reasons and comply promptly), Rule 4 (setting aside orders); Section 151 (inherent powers); Order XLIII (appeals from interim orders).
  • Specific Relief Act, 1963, provisions on perpetual and temporary injunctions and circumstances where injunction is refused.
  • Indian Partnership Act, 1932, including provisions on partnership property, dissolution and accounts, and the restriction on suits by unregistered firms in certain cases.
  • Transfer of Property Act, 1882, Section 52 (transfer of property pending suit), noting State amendments in some States.

VPossible steps, in sequence

  1. Establish the legal basis for claiming a right in the premises. Use of premises by the firm, by itself, does not make immovable property partnership property; analyse title, acquisition documents, contribution of funds, the partnership deed and relevant evidence.
  2. File a suit (for accounts, dissolution or declaration as appropriate) because an injunction normally requires an underlying claim.
  3. File the injunction application with the suit, stating all material facts, including facts that go against the applicant, and explaining urgency.
  4. If real and imminent harm is shown, ask for an ad interim order on the first hearing with notice to the other side. Comply with the procedural requirements for any order without notice.
  5. Be ready to give undertakings, including about damages if the injunction later proves unjustified.
  6. Track the property records for any attempted registration. Consider a caveat where applicable.
  7. If the order is refused or granted, consider the appeal route under Order XLIII.

VIDocuments and evidence

Partnership deed or evidence of partnership; title documents; proof of use of premises by the firm; accounts and bank statements; agreement or correspondence showing the intended sale; evidence of urgency (dates, messages, notices).

VIIProceedings and forum

Civil court with territorial and pecuniary jurisdiction; commercial court division where the dispute qualifies as commercial. Where a partnership deed has an arbitration clause, consider interim relief under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (Section 9) instead.

VIIIHow it could go

  • If the applicant proves the three elements and acted promptly, the court may order status quo or restrain alienation pending trial.
  • If damages are adequate or the applicant delayed or withheld facts, the court may refuse.
  • A conditional order (such as a deposit or undertaking) may be passed.

No result is assured.

IXMisconceptions and lessons

"An injunction is automatic if I am right." It is discretionary and depends on the three elements and conduct. "I can wait and see." Delay is often held against the applicant. Candour matters; hiding adverse facts can lose the relief.

XCommon questions

Can I get an injunction before filing a suit?

Generally the application is made within a suit; urgent steps and caveats may be possible but need careful advice.

What if the property is sold anyway?

The effect on a purchaser depends on the facts and on the rule on transfers pending suit.

XIWhat this scenario does not cover

Injunctions in IP cases, anti-suit injunctions, injunctions against government.

XIIPrimary sources to consult

CPC 1908; Specific Relief Act 1963; Partnership Act 1932; Transfer of Property Act 1882; official repository for authority.
