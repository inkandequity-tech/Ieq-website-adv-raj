Sudden Dismissal of a Manager

What can an employee do after a sudden dismissal, and does it matter whether they are a "worker" or a manager?

Illustrative scenario. This is a hypothetical illustrative scenario prepared for general legal information. It does not describe an actual client matter or guarantee any particular outcome. The applicable law and available remedies depend on the facts and jurisdiction; this page is not legal advice.

IHypothetical facts

A fictional private company dismisses a mid-level manager with immediate effect, alleging misconduct. No charge sheet, inquiry or opportunity to respond was given. The appointment letter provides a notice period and salary in lieu. Final dues have not been paid. The manager supervises a team, approves leave and works mostly on administrative and managerial tasks. The company is located in Uttar Pradesh.

IIThe practical problem

The manager needs to know whether to claim reinstatement or compensation, which forum applies, and the time limit. The employer wants to know its exposure.

IIIIssues

  1. Does the manager fall within the statutory definition of a worker, or only under contract law?
  2. What do the appointment letter and policies say on misconduct, inquiry and notice?
  3. Which labour framework now applies, given the 2025 commencement of the labour codes?
  4. Recovery of wages and dues.
  5. Whether reinstatement is available.
  6. Restrictive covenants and post-termination obligations.

IVLegal framework

  • Labour codes: the Ministry of Labour and Employment states that the Code on Wages, 2019, Industrial Relations Code, 2020, Code on Social Security, 2020 and Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 came into force on 21 November 2025. For this Uttar Pradesh scenario, verify applicable Central/State rules, notifications, establishment coverage and the law governing events that occurred before commencement. Official sources include https://labour.gov.in/sites/default/files/pib2200433.pdf and https://labour.gov.in/sites/default/files/e-noti-ir-_0.pdf.
  • Industrial Relations Code, 2020: the definition of "worker" and its exclusions, including for employees working mainly in a managerial, administrative or supervisory capacity and any wage ceiling.
  • Industrial Relations Code provisions on retrenchment, standing orders (which apply to establishments above a stated size), grievance redressal, conciliation and the Industrial Tribunal, and the time limit for raising a dispute.
  • Code on Wages, 2019: timing of payment of wages and full and final dues on termination.
  • Code on Social Security, 2020: gratuity and other benefits and eligibility.
  • State shops and establishments legislation, if it continues to apply to the establishment.
  • Indian Contract Act, 1872: breach of contract and damages; Section 27 on restraint of trade as it affects non-compete clauses.
  • Specific Relief Act, 1963: contracts of personal service are generally not specifically enforceable, subject to recognised exceptions.
  • Natural justice in private employment is limited compared with public employment, and depends on the contract, policies, standing orders and statute.

VPossible steps, in sequence

  1. Classify the employee: worker under the Industrial Relations Code or not, based on duties, wages and the statutory definition.
  2. Collect the appointment letter, policies, termination letter and payroll records.
  3. Send a written request for reasons, the basis of the allegation and payment of dues.
  4. If a worker, consider the statutory grievance and conciliation route and then the Industrial Tribunal, mindful of the time limit. If not a worker, consider a contractual claim for notice pay, unpaid wages and damages in a civil court, and a claim for statutory dues under the wages and social security frameworks.
  5. Evaluate settlement, including payment of dues, a reference letter, and treatment of any restrictive covenants.
  6. Employer side: review the evidence of misconduct, whether any inquiry should have been held under the policy or standing orders, and the exposure on notice pay and interest.

VIDocuments and evidence

Appointment letter; policies and handbook; standing orders if any; termination letter; emails; payslips; bank statements; evidence of duties and reporting lines; performance records; any warning letters.

VIIProceedings and forum

Conciliation officer and Industrial Tribunal for worker disputes under the Industrial Relations Code; civil court for contractual claims of a non-worker; authorities under the wages and social security frameworks for specified dues.

VIIIHow it could go

  • If the employee is a worker, a tribunal may examine the fairness of the termination and can grant remedies, which may include reinstatement or compensation depending on the facts.
  • If the employee is not a worker, remedies are generally contractual: notice pay, accrued dues, damages for breach, and statutory benefits.
  • A negotiated separation is a common practical outcome.

No result is assured.

IXMisconceptions and lessons

"Every dismissed employee can get reinstated." It depends on the employee's category and the forum. "No inquiry means automatic reinstatement." Not in every case. Dispute time limits can be short. Employers should keep inquiry records; employees should keep copies of all communications.

XCommon questions

Is a manager covered by the same law as a factory worker?

Not necessarily; the statutory definition of worker decides which framework applies.

What if my final dues are not paid?

Statutory and contractual routes exist, and time limits apply.

XIWhat this scenario does not cover

Public-sector employees, sexual harassment complaints, whistle-blowing, gig workers, collective bargaining.

XIIPrimary sources to consult

The four labour codes and their rules; Gazette notifications on commencement and repeal; Uttar Pradesh State rules; Indian Contract Act 1872; Specific Relief Act 1963; official repository for authority.
