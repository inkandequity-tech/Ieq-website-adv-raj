How does the Right to Information Act work now, and how can a refusal be challenged?

Illustrative scenario. This is a hypothetical illustrative scenario prepared for general legal information. It does not describe an actual client matter or guarantee any particular outcome. The applicable law and available remedies depend on the facts and jurisdiction; this page is not legal advice.

IHypothetical facts

A fictional unsuccessful bidder files an RTI request with a public authority for the evaluation sheet, the names of evaluation committee members and the winning bidder's technical bid. The Public Information Officer refuses, citing personal information and commercial confidence, without detailed reasons.

IIThe practical problem

Understanding what can be obtained, how to appeal, and how the recent amendment affects personal-information requests.

IIIIssues

  1. Exemptions for commercial confidence and personal information.
  2. The effect of the amendment to the personal-information exemption.
  3. Severability and partial disclosure.
  4. The duty to give reasons.
  5. Time limits for appeals.
  6. Remedies if the Commission does not decide.

IVLegal framework

  • Right to Information Act, 2005: verify the current text of Sections 6, 7, 8, 10, 19 and 20, including response and appeal periods, as amended and commenced. Consider Section 11 third-party procedure where applicable. Do not promise disclosure of bid records: commercial confidence, third-party interests, public interest and severability require a record-specific analysis.
  • DPDP Act, 2023, Section 44(3): verify the official commencement notification and exact substituted text of RTI Act Section 8(1)(j).
  • State RTI rules and fee requirements.
  • Proactive disclosure duties of public authorities (Section 4) and tender transparency rules in procurement law.

VPossible steps, in sequence

  1. Draft the request narrowly and specifically; ask for records, not opinions. Pay the fee as required.
  2. Note the date of the request and the response deadline.
  3. If refused, file the first appeal within the statutory period, arguing for partial disclosure and pointing to the absence of reasons.
  4. If the first appeal fails or is not decided, file the second appeal to the Information Commission within its period.
  5. Consider tender-specific remedies in parallel, such as a challenge to the award, through the proper forum.
  6. If delay or non-compliance persists, consider remedies through the High Court.

VIDocuments and evidence

RTI application and proof of fee and delivery; refusal letter; first appeal and decision; tender notice and bid documents already available.

VIIProceedings and forum

Public Information Officer; first appellate authority; Central or State Information Commission; High Court under Article 226.

VIIIHow it could go

  • Disclosure of evaluation scores and award reasons depends on the records, applicable exemptions, third-party procedure and current authority; seek severable disclosure where appropriate.
  • Personal information may be withheld where the current statutory exemption applies; apply the amended text and binding authority rather than assuming a blanket rule.
  • Partial disclosure with redaction is a common result.

No result is assured.

IXMisconceptions and lessons

"RTI gives access to everything." Exemptions apply. Appeal deadlines are fixed; diary them.

XCommon questions

Can I get my competitor's bid?

Commercial secrets are protected, but some tender records may be disclosable.

Where do I appeal?

First to the appellate authority in the same public authority, then to the Information Commission.

XIWhat this scenario does not cover

Information requests about private bodies, security and intelligence organisations, judicial records.

XIIPrimary sources to consult

RTI Act 2005 as amended; DPDP Act 2023 Section 44(3); State RTI rules; Commission decisions and official repository.
