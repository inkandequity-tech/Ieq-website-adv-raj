Founder Deadlock in a Private Company

Co-founders who cannot agree and hold equal shares want to know what the law provides and what the documents may already say.

The applicable law and available remedies depend on the facts and jurisdiction; this page is not legal advice.

IHypothetical facts

A fictional private limited company has two founders, each holding half the shares and each a director. A shareholders' agreement and articles of association exist. The board cannot pass a budget or approve hiring because each founder votes against the other's proposals. One founder has stopped attending meetings; the other is paying vendors without board approval. The shareholders' agreement includes an escalation clause and an arbitration clause but no buy-sell mechanism. Business performance is falling.

IIThe practical problem

Keep the business running, avoid a value-destroying fight, and understand whether to negotiate an exit, invoke the contract, or go to a Tribunal.

IIIIssues

  1. What the shareholders' agreement and articles say about deadlock, reserved matters and exit.
  2. Whether deadlock alone amounts to oppression or mismanagement.
  3. Whether the arbitration clause covers the dispute and whether statutory Tribunal remedies can be arbitrated.
  4. Eligibility to apply to the Tribunal.
  5. Interim protection.
  6. Valuation and mechanics of a buy-out.

IVLegal framework

  • Companies Act, 2013, Sections 241 and 242. Eligible members may apply to the National Company Law Tribunal for relief where the statutory grounds for oppression and mismanagement are made out. A 50:50 deadlock or disagreement over management is not, by itself, automatic proof of oppression or mismanagement. The statutory tests, including the relevant prejudice/oppression requirements and the just-and-equitable winding-up condition where applicable, must be applied to the facts and the precise subsection relied on. Section 242 gives the Tribunal specified remedial powers, including orders regulating the company's affairs or share purchase in appropriate cases; relief is not guaranteed.
  • Companies Act, 2013, Section 244. Standing to apply is subject to the statutory member-number or shareholding thresholds, the precise category of company and any applicable exclusion. The Tribunal may waive the requirements in circumstances permitted by the section. Confirm eligibility, the current text and any required waiver application before asserting that a particular founder can maintain a petition.
  • Companies Act, 2013, Section 271(1)(e) concerns winding up by the Tribunal on just and equitable grounds. It is a distinct statutory remedy, not an automatic consequence of a deadlock or a substitute for proving the elements of an oppression-and-mismanagement petition. Winding up can destroy business value and should not be presented as the expected outcome. Check the current statutory framework, procedural route and relevant binding judgments.
  • Articles of association and shareholders' agreement. The Companies Act and the articles govern the company's internal constitution; a shareholders' agreement ordinarily binds its parties under contract law. A provision in a shareholders' agreement that conflicts with the Act or the articles may raise enforceability and implementation issues, particularly where company action or third-party effect is involved.
  • Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, including Section 8, and Companies Act provisions governing the NCLT. A valid arbitration clause may cover contractual disputes between shareholders, but it does not mean that every dispute involving a company can be referred to arbitration. Statutory oppression-and-mismanagement remedies and other matters reserved to the NCLT may be non-arbitrable; the analysis depends on the substance of the relief, parties, statutory scheme and binding precedent. Do not promise that arbitration and NCLT proceedings can freely be split or pursued in parallel. Obtain forum-specific advice before filing.
  • Companies Act, 2013, Section 247 and the applicable Registered Valuers rules apply where a valuation is required under the relevant statutory provision. They do not make a registered valuer compulsory for every negotiated private-company share transfer or buy-out. The valuation method may instead be governed by the agreement, transaction terms, applicable law, tax rules, FEMA rules or the Tribunal's order. Identify the source of any claimed valuation requirement.
  • Companies Act, 2013, Section 56 and the applicable rules govern relevant share-transfer formalities. Stamp duty must be checked under the law in force for the instrument and transaction. If a non-resident is involved, review FEMA and the applicable non-debt instrument rules, including pricing, sectoral limits, reporting and payment requirements. Transfer restrictions, pre-emption rights, board approvals and company records must also be checked against the articles and agreement.
  • Common contractual deadlock tools (not statutory): escalation to chairs or advisers; mediation; casting vote; independent director; put or call options; "shotgun" or "Texas shoot-out" buy-sell clauses; third-party valuation. Whether any of these exists must be read from the documents.

VPossible steps, in sequence

  1. Read the shareholders' agreement, articles and any side letters. List reserved matters, escalation steps, notice requirements, dispute-resolution clauses and transfer restrictions.
  2. Assess the company's operational needs. Identify decisions that must be made to keep the business lawful and solvent (statutory filings, tax payments, payroll).
  3. Follow the contractual escalation steps in order, keeping written records. Skipping a required step can be raised against the party that skipped it.
  4. Explore a negotiated solution: independent mediation; interim governance protocol; buy-out with independent valuation; staged exit; sale of the company to a third party.
  5. Obtain a forum analysis before commencing proceedings. Arbitration may be appropriate for arbitrable contractual claims covered by a valid clause; statutory remedies reserved to the NCLT may not be arbitrable. Do not assume a party can elect to divide a single dispute across forums or that parallel proceedings are harmless. Analyse parties, causes of action, relief sought, the statutory scheme and current binding authority.
  6. If the Tribunal route is considered, confirm the applicant's eligibility, assemble evidence of conduct (not merely disagreement), and consider an interim application where the company or the applicant faces real harm.
  7. If a buy-out is agreed, document it with valuation basis, payment terms, release, resignations, non-compete scope (subject to enforceability), and transfer formalities including stamp duty and company filings.

VIDocuments and evidence

Memorandum and articles; shareholders' agreement and amendments; board and general meeting notices and minutes; statutory registers; financial statements and bank statements; tax and compliance filings; correspondence recording the disagreements; evidence of unauthorised actions or exclusion; any valuation reports.

VIIProceedings and forum

Negotiation and mediation; arbitration only for disputes covered by a valid arbitration agreement and legally capable of arbitration; NCLT for statutory company-law relief within its jurisdiction. Section 8 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act may be relevant to a court proceeding involving an arbitration agreement, but its application does not convert a non-arbitrable statutory claim into an arbitrable one. NCLT territorial allocation, procedure and any appeal to NCLAT must be checked under the current Act, rules and notifications.

VIIIHow it could go

  • A structured buy-out at an independently determined value is a common commercial resolution where both sides prefer to preserve value.
  • A Tribunal may regulate conduct or order a buy-out if oppression or mismanagement is established; mere disagreement may not be enough.
  • If arbitration is held to cover the dispute, the Tribunal route may be limited, or the two proceedings may run in parallel with practical complications.

No result is assured.

IXMisconceptions and lessons

  • "A 50:50 split means the Tribunal will automatically order a buy-out." It will not; the statutory tests apply.
  • "The shareholders' agreement automatically overrides the articles." It does not; the Companies Act, articles, agreement and relevant case law must be read together, and inconsistencies can create enforceability or implementation problems.
  • "I can transfer my shares freely." Transfer restrictions in the articles or agreement may apply.

A well-drafted deadlock clause, with a clear valuation and exit mechanism, is far easier to use than any statutory remedy. Founders should address this at incorporation.

XCommon questions

Can I force the other founder to buy me out?

Only if the documents provide for it or a competent forum orders it on the statutory grounds.

Is deadlock itself a ground for relief?

It can be relevant but is not automatically sufficient; the legal tests depend on the facts.

Which comes first, arbitration or the Tribunal?

It depends on the clause wording and the nature of the claim; this is a point requiring careful legal analysis.

XIWhat this scenario does not cover

Listed companies; foreign shareholders; fraud allegations; insolvency of the company; investor protective rights in venture financing.

XIIPrimary sources to consult

India Code: Companies Act 2013 (Sections 56, 241, 242, 244, 247, 271 and relevant rules); Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996; Indian Stamp Act and the relevant State amendments; FEMA rules if a non-resident is involved. NCLT and NCLAT official websites for current rules and practice directions.
