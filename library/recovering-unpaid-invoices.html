Recovering Unpaid Invoices

A business owed money by a customer wants to know what legal routes exist to recover it, in what order, and how limitation affects them.

Illustrative scenario. This is a hypothetical illustrative scenario prepared for general legal information. It does not describe an actual client matter or guarantee any particular outcome. The applicable law and available remedies depend on the facts and jurisdiction; this page is not legal advice.

IHypothetical facts

A fictional manufacturing supplier ("the Supplier") delivered packaging material to a fictional trading company ("the Buyer") over three consecutive months under purchase orders and invoices. The invoices stated payment within 30 days and a late-payment interest rate. The Buyer paid about half, then paid nothing further. About eight months after the last delivery, the Buyer's accounts manager emailed: "We acknowledge the balance is outstanding and will clear it next quarter." Nothing was paid. Two follow-up emails went unanswered. There is no single master agreement; the dealings are documented by purchase orders, invoices, delivery challans and emails. One of the Buyer's cheques, given for part of the balance, was returned unpaid.

IIThe practical problem

  • Recover the balance and interest without losing the commercial relationship if that is still desirable.
  • Choose between slower, costly litigation and quicker pressure routes.
  • Avoid losing the claim to limitation while negotiating.

IIIIssues

  1. Is the debt provable from the documents?
  2. Has limitation run, or has it been extended by acknowledgment or part payment?
  3. Is the claim a "commercial dispute", and is mandatory pre-institution mediation required?
  4. Is a summary suit available?
  5. Does the cheque create a separate criminal remedy?
  6. Is the Supplier a micro or small enterprise, opening a statutory payment route?
  7. Can interim relief be justified?

IVLegal framework

  • Limitation Act, 1963. Articles 14 and 15 generally provide a three-year period for specified claims for the price of goods sold and delivered, with the starting point depending on whether a fixed credit period was agreed. Classify each invoice and identify its own accrual date; do not assume every invoice has the same limitation date. Section 18 requires a written and signed acknowledgment of liability made before the existing limitation period expires. Section 19 concerns qualifying part-payment made before expiry and requires compliance with the statutory conditions, including the required written acknowledgment of payment. An email may qualify depending on its content, attribution/signature and the applicable law of electronic records; do not assume that every email restarts limitation. Acknowledgment or payment after limitation has expired does not ordinarily revive the claim under Sections 18 or 19. Section 3 requires dismissal of a time-barred suit even if limitation is not pleaded.
  • Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, Order XXXVII (summary suits). A summary suit may be available where the claim falls within the categories specified by the Order, including certain debts or liquidated demands arising from a written contract. It is not automatic merely because invoices exist: examine the contractual documents, whether liability and amount are sufficiently ascertainable, the applicable court and the pleaded claim. Under the prescribed procedure, a defendant served with summons for judgment must seek leave to defend within the applicable period; the court decides whether leave is unconditional, conditional or refused under the governing principles. Verify the current Order and applicable High Court amendments/rules before stating a deadline.
  • Commercial Courts Act, 2015. A dispute arising from the sale of goods or supply of services may be a commercial dispute. The specified-value threshold is generally not less than INR 3 lakh, subject to the Act, applicable notifications and the valuation rules in force for the relevant forum. Section 12A generally requires pre-institution mediation for a suit that does not contemplate any urgent interim relief; the Supreme Court has treated this requirement as mandatory for covered suits. A party should not label relief "urgent" merely to bypass mediation: the court may examine the substance of the request. Check the current statute, rules and local commercial-court practice, including the relevant valuation and territorial allocation.
  • Identify the contractual basis for interest and whether the clause is enforceable on the facts. The Interest Act, 1978 and CPC Section 34 may be relevant to interest for periods before suit, during suit and after decree; the legal basis and rate differ by period. Pre-suit interest may arise under contract, statute, usage or other applicable law; pendente lite and post-decree interest are subject to the court's statutory powers and discretion.
  • Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, Section 138. A cheque-dishonour complaint is a separate statutory remedy and its prerequisites must be strictly checked. In general, the payee or holder must issue a written demand notice within 30 days of receiving information from the bank about dishonour; the drawer then has 15 days after receipt of the notice to pay. If payment is not made, the cause of action ordinarily arises after that 15-day period, and the complaint must generally be filed within one month of that cause of action, subject to the court's power to condone delay for sufficient cause. Check the statutory text and current binding decisions for the facts, service, presentation, jurisdiction and any applicable procedural issue. A civil recovery claim and a Section 138 complaint are distinct; one does not automatically substitute for the other.
  • Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development Act, 2006, Sections 15–18. If the Supplier qualifies as a micro or small supplier for the relevant transactions and the statutory conditions are met, Section 15 regulates the agreed payment period (which cannot exceed 45 days from acceptance or deemed acceptance), Section 16 provides compound interest with monthly rests at three times the RBI-notified bank rate, and Sections 17–18 provide for recovery and reference to the Micro and Small Enterprises Facilitation Council. Eligibility, the effect and timing of Udyam registration, the nature of the supply, buyer status and applicable judicial decisions must be checked for the particular facts; do not assume retrospective coverage or eligibility from registration alone.
  • Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. An operational creditor may issue a demand notice and, where statutory requirements are met, apply for initiation of the corporate insolvency resolution process. The minimum default threshold must be checked against the applicable notification and current law; do not insert a threshold without date-specific verification. A genuine dispute that existed before receipt of the demand notice, and other statutory defects, may prevent admission. The Code is a collective insolvency-resolution mechanism, not an ordinary debt-collection or coercive recovery device; it should not be threatened or used without a proper factual and legal basis.

VPossible steps, in sequence

  1. Assemble the evidence (see checklist below) and compute the principal, interest and the limitation date for each invoice.
  2. Diary the limitation date separately for every invoice and record the legal basis for any claimed extension. A qualifying acknowledgment under Section 18 or part-payment under Section 19 must satisfy the statutory conditions and occur before the existing period expires. Do not assume a verbal promise, a post-expiry email or an ordinary ledger entry revives a time-barred claim.
  3. Send a clear written demand or legal notice quoting invoice numbers, amounts, interest clause and a reasonable deadline. A notice is not a legal precondition for a plain civil suit but it supports interest, shows good faith, and may prompt payment.
  4. If cheques were returned, decide separately whether to pursue the statutory cheque remedy within its strict time limits.
  5. If the Supplier may qualify as a micro or small supplier for these transactions, verify eligibility and registration timing, then evaluate the statutory facilitation route under Sections 15–18 of the MSMED Act.
  6. Check whether pre-institution mediation is required, and if so complete it before filing.
  7. File a summary suit if the claim fits Order XXXVII, or an ordinary or commercial suit otherwise.
  8. Seek interim relief only where evidence supports the applicable legal test. Attachment before judgment under CPC Order XXXVIII Rule 5 is exceptional; the claimant must establish the statutory conditions, including a real intention to obstruct or delay execution by disposing of or removing property. A debt, non-payment or suspicion alone is not enough. Identify the evidence and the applicable binding authority before seeking this remedy.
  9. After a decree, execution proceedings (CPC Order XXI) are a separate stage with their own practical difficulties.

VIDocuments and evidence

Purchase orders; invoices with payment terms and interest clause; delivery challans and e-way bills; proof of receipt by the Buyer; account statements or ledger; GST returns reflecting the invoices; all emails and messages, including the acknowledgment; returned cheques and bank memos; any terms of business or standard conditions and any jurisdiction or arbitration clause; micro or small enterprise registration certificate, if applicable.

VIIProceedings and forum

Pre-suit notice where strategically useful or legally required; pre-institution mediation under Commercial Courts Act Section 12A where applicable; then the court with territorial and pecuniary jurisdiction. CPC Section 20 is the usual general provision for suits against defendants or where the cause of action arises, subject to the facts and other applicable provisions. CPC Section 19 is specifically concerned with compensation for wrongs to persons or movable property and should not be cited as the general rule for invoice recovery. A jurisdiction clause may select one among courts that already have jurisdiction; it cannot ordinarily confer jurisdiction on a court that otherwise lacks it. Confirm the commercial-court forum and specified value where applicable. Check whether an arbitration clause in the invoices or terms sends the dispute to arbitration instead of court.

VIIIHow it could go

  • If the documents are clean and no genuine defence is raised, a summary suit may be a comparatively focused route. If leave to defend is granted, the matter proceeds as an ordinary suit.
  • If the Buyer raises a real dispute about quality or quantity, the claim becomes a fuller trial. The Supplier's proof of delivery and acceptance becomes central.
  • If the claim is time-barred on some invoices, only the in-time invoices may be recoverable.
  • If the Buyer settles after a notice or mediation, the Supplier should document the settlement in a signed agreement with a default clause.

No result is assured, and timelines vary widely by court.

IXMisconceptions and lessons

  • "A legal notice stops limitation." It does not.
  • "An email promise always extends limitation." Whether an email satisfies the statutory acknowledgment requirement depends on its wording and authorship.
  • "Insolvency is a quick way to get paid." It carries serious consequences and is often unsuitable.

Put credit terms, interest, and the dispute-resolution clause in writing before the first delivery. Keep proof of delivery. Diary limitation per invoice.

XCommon questions

How long do I have to sue for unpaid invoices?

Time limits depend on the type of claim and the dates involved; a general educational answer is that limitation is measured from specific events, so each invoice should be checked separately.

Is a legal notice compulsory before filing a money suit?

Not for an ordinary civil suit, though it is useful. Some statutory remedies, such as the cheque remedy, require a notice within strict time limits.

Can I claim interest?

Often yes, depending on the contract, statute and court's discretion.

XIWhat this scenario does not cover

Cross-border sales, tax treatment of write-offs, disputes involving government buyers, and arbitration seated outside India.
