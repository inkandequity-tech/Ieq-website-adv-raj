Two Similar Brand Names in the Same Category

What can a registered trademark owner do against a similar mark, and what risk does a new brand face?

Illustrative scenario. This is a hypothetical illustrative scenario prepared for general legal information. It does not describe an actual client matter or guarantee any particular outcome. The applicable law and available remedies depend on the facts and jurisdiction; this page is not legal advice.

IHypothetical facts

A fictional established company has a registered word mark for packaged snacks. A new fictional company launches a snack brand with a name that sounds similar and uses a similar colour scheme. The older company also notices the new brand's pending application in the trade marks journal.

IIThe practical problem

The older company wants to protect its brand. The newer company, after receiving a cease-and-desist letter, wants to know whether it can keep the name.

IIIIssues

  1. Likelihood of confusion between the marks, considering sound, appearance, goods, trade channels and consumer type.
  2. Registration status and priority of use.
  3. Infringement versus passing off.
  4. Opposition to the pending application.
  5. Defences, such as prior use, honest concurrent use, or descriptiveness.
  6. Interim relief.

IVLegal framework

  • Trade Marks Act, 1999: grounds for refusal (Sections 9 and 11); infringement (Section 29); passing off preserved (Section 27(2)); opposition (Section 21); rectification and removal (Sections 47 and 57); jurisdiction for suits (Section 134); reliefs (Section 135).
  • Trade Marks Rules, 2017: opposition and counter-statement procedure. Under Section 21 of the Trade Marks Act, opposition is ordinarily to be filed within four months from the date of advertisement or re-advertisement of the application; the counter-statement deadline and consequences of default are governed by the Act and Rules.
  • Since 2021, rectification and appeal functions formerly with the Intellectual Property Appellate Board were moved to the High Courts under the Tribunals Reforms framework.
  • Commercial Courts Act, 2015: IP disputes are commercial disputes; pre-institution mediation under Section 12A has an exception where urgent interim relief is contemplated.
  • Principles of deceptive similarity, anti-dissection, and the standard of the average consumer of imperfect recollection.

VPossible steps, in sequence

For the owner

  1. Gather registration certificates, evidence of use and reputation (sales, advertising, dates).
  2. Compare marks and goods; document instances of actual confusion if any.
  3. Decide between a notice, an opposition in the Registry, a suit for infringement and passing off, or a combination.
  4. If suing, plan an interim injunction application supported by evidence of use, reputation and the risk of confusion.

For the new adopter

  1. Evaluate the claim honestly with a search of the register and common law use.
  2. Consider modifying the mark, coexistence terms, or opposing or defending as appropriate.
  3. If suit is filed, assess defences and the cost of rebranding versus defending.

VIDocuments and evidence

Registration certificates; journal extracts; evidence of first and continuous use; invoices, advertising and sales data; samples of both marks and packaging; correspondence; search reports.

VIIProceedings and forum

Trade Marks Registry (opposition, application stage); High Court or the commercial court with jurisdiction for infringement and passing off; rectification before the High Court.

VIIIHow it could go

  • Courts compare the marks as a whole in context. A finding of deceptive similarity may support an injunction; a finding of differences in goods or marks may not.
  • Coexistence agreements, with territory, class and presentation limits, are a common commercial outcome.
  • Pending applications may be refused or limited following opposition.

No result is assured.

IXMisconceptions and lessons

"Registration of my company name protects my brand." Company registration is not trademark registration. "A different colour is enough." Courts consider the overall impression. Clearance searches before launch cost far less than rebranding.

XCommon questions

What is the difference between infringement and passing off?

Infringement protects a registered mark under the statute; passing off protects goodwill and reputation, including for unregistered marks.

How long do I have to oppose an application?

Section 21 ordinarily provides four months from advertisement or re-advertisement. Verify the current statute, rules and the specific Registry record immediately; do not wait until the last day.

XIWhat this scenario does not cover

Well-known mark status, international filings, domain-name disputes, counterfeits and border measures.

XIIPrimary sources to consult

Trade Marks Act 1999; Trade Marks Rules 2017; Registry website; High Court websites.
