Who Owns a Logo Made by a Freelancer?

Who owns the copyright in commissioned creative work, and what can the creator or commissioner do?

Illustrative scenario. This is a hypothetical illustrative scenario prepared for general legal information. It does not describe an actual client matter or guarantee any particular outcome. The applicable law and available remedies depend on the facts and jurisdiction; this page is not legal advice.

IHypothetical facts

A fictional freelance designer creates a logo for a fictional company on the basis of emails and a single invoice. There is no written assignment. The company later uses the logo on products, packaging and in a national campaign, and registers it as a trademark application in the company's name. The designer says only a limited use was agreed.

IIThe practical problem

The company needs certainty of ownership for its brand. The designer wants fair compensation or recognition for use beyond what was agreed.

IIIIssues

  1. Who is the first owner of copyright in a commissioned work by an independent contractor?
  2. Is there an assignment or only a licence, and is it in writing?
  3. Scope, territory and duration of any assignment.
  4. Moral rights.
  5. Interaction with the trademark application.
  6. Infringement and remedies.

IVLegal framework

  • Copyright Act, 1957: definition of artistic work and author; first ownership under Section 17; assignment under Sections 18 and 19; licences under Section 30; author's special rights under Section 57; infringement under Section 51; civil remedies under Section 55; and jurisdiction under Section 62. The statutory commissioned-work exception is limited to specified categories and should not be assumed to transfer copyright in every commissioned logo. A logo created by an independent freelancer will ordinarily require analysis of authorship, employment status, any valid written assignment, and any express or implied licence. Section 19 contains default rules where duration or territory is omitted; verify the current text and facts before stating the precise consequence.
  • Whether a logo qualifies as an "artistic work" and the effect on commissioned-work rules.
  • Implied licence principles where payment was made and use was contemplated.
  • Trade Marks Act, 1999: trademark registration does not by itself resolve copyright ownership in the artwork.
  • Limitation Act, 1963 for civil claims.

VPossible steps, in sequence

For the designer

  1. Collect emails, drafts, invoices and dates showing creation and the agreed use.
  2. Analyse whether any assignment exists and, if not, whether an implied licence limits the company's use.
  3. Send a measured notice stating the claim, proposing a licence fee or a proper assignment, and preserving rights.
  4. If unresolved, consider an appropriately framed copyright claim and, separately, assess whether any opposition or other step in the trademark proceedings is legally available on its own statutory grounds. A copyright ownership dispute does not automatically invalidate a trademark application.

For the company

  1. Check whether a written assignment exists; if not, negotiate a signed assignment with scope, territory, duration and consideration.
  2. Record the chain of title and update the brand portfolio.
  3. Assess exposure before launching further campaigns.

VIDocuments and evidence

Emails and briefs; drafts and the final artwork file with dates; invoice and payment proof; any terms of engagement; evidence of the company's uses; the trademark application and filing particulars.

VIIProceedings and forum

Negotiation and mediation; civil or commercial court for infringement, with jurisdiction rules under the Copyright Act; Trade Marks Registry or High Court for trademark-related steps.

VIIIHow it could go

  • If the designer is an independent contractor and there is no effective assignment, the designer will generally be the first owner, subject to the exact statutory category, contractual terms and facts. The company may have an express or implied licence, whose scope cannot be assumed without examining the communications, payment, brief and intended use.
  • Courts may treat payment and the contemplated use as shaping the scope of licence. Extensions beyond that may be negotiated.
  • A signed assignment, even after the event, can resolve the position.

No result is assured.

IXMisconceptions and lessons

"Paying the invoice transfers all rights." Not necessarily. "Registering the logo as a trademark gives ownership of the artwork." It does not. Agree ownership, territory, duration and credit in a signed agreement before work begins.

XCommon questions

Does a freelancer or the client own the logo?

It depends on the written terms and the legal rules on first ownership and assignment.

Is an email enough to assign copyright?

Section 19 requires a written assignment signed by the assignor. Whether a particular electronic record and signature satisfy the applicable legal requirements is fact-specific; do not assume an ordinary email alone transfers copyright.

XIWhat this scenario does not cover

Employee-created works, software copyright, AI-generated works, performers' rights.

XIIPrimary sources to consult

Copyright Act 1957; Trade Marks Act 1999; official repository for authority on commissioned works and implied licences.
