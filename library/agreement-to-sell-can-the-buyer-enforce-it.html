Agreement to Sell: Can the Buyer Enforce It?

A buyer under an agreement to sell wants to know if they can force the sale or must accept a refund.

The applicable law and available remedies depend on the facts and jurisdiction; this page is not legal advice.

IHypothetical facts

A fictional buyer ("the Buyer") signs a written agreement to buy a house from a fictional owner ("the Seller") for a stated price. The Buyer pays a substantial advance. The agreement sets a date for completion within a few months, requires the Seller to deliver clear title documents, and requires the Buyer to pay the balance on completion. Before the date, the Buyer hears the Seller has been negotiating with another buyer at a higher price. The Seller has not yet delivered the title documents.

IIThe practical problem

Does the Buyer have a right to the house itself, or only to money? What must the Buyer show to a court? What if the Seller sells to a third party first?

IIIIssues

  1. Whether the agreement is enforceable and what it says about time.
  2. The Buyer's continuous readiness and willingness.
  3. The Seller's title and ability to perform.
  4. The effect of a sale to a third party who may or may not have notice.
  5. Alternative relief of refund of the advance with compensation.
  6. Registration and stamp duty effects on admissibility.
  7. Limitation.

IVLegal framework

  • Specific Relief Act, 1963, particularly Sections 10, 14 and 16. The 2018 amendment materially changed the statutory approach to specific performance, but relief is not automatic: the contract must be enforceable, no statutory bar may apply, and the plaintiff must satisfy the applicable requirements. Section 16 includes personal bars, including the requirement to aver and prove performance or continuous readiness and willingness to perform the essential terms the plaintiff must perform, except terms whose performance the defendant has prevented or waived. Assess the actual agreement, reciprocal obligations and evidence; do not state that an advance payment alone establishes readiness and willingness.
  • Specific Relief Act, 1963, Section 19, including Section 19(b). Specific performance may, subject to the statutory exceptions, be enforced against a person claiming under a party to the contract, including a subsequent transferee. An important exception protects a subsequent transferee for value who paid in good faith and without notice of the original contract. The facts, consideration, good faith and notice must be established under the applicable evidentiary rules and binding precedent; do not say that every later purchaser is automatically bound or automatically protected.
  • Specific Relief Act, 1963, Section 21 (compensation in addition to or in substitution for specific performance, where legally available) and Section 22 (possession, partition or refund of earnest money/deposit in the circumstances covered by the section). The plaintiff should specifically claim appropriate ancillary or alternative relief in the pleadings and seek amendment if needed. Refund or compensation is not guaranteed merely because specific performance is refused; entitlement depends on the contract, pleadings, evidence and statutory requirements.
  • Transfer of Property Act, 1882. Section 54 provides that a contract for sale does not, of itself, create any interest in or charge on the property; a completed sale of immovable property must satisfy the applicable conveyance and registration requirements. Section 53A may protect possession in part performance only when its statutory conditions are met, including the applicable written-contract and registration requirements. Section 52 (lis pendens) may affect transfers made during pending litigation; verify the State-specific amendments and the effect of the doctrine on the proposed transaction.
  • Registration Act, 1908, Sections 17 and 49, read with the applicable amendments and State law. Whether an agreement to sell requires registration depends on its terms, possession, intended legal effect and the applicable statutory provisions. Section 49's proviso may permit an unregistered document to be received as evidence of a contract in a suit for specific performance or for a collateral transaction, subject to the statute and other admissibility rules. Registration, admissibility and stamp-duty compliance are distinct questions; do not treat an agreement as admissible merely because it is unregistered, or assume that an evidentiary exception cures deficient stamp duty.
  • State stamp law. Stamp duty on an agreement to sell varies by State and by whether possession is delivered. An insufficiently stamped agreement may be impounded and may need duty and penalty before being admitted in evidence.
  • Limitation Act, 1963, Article 54. The limitation period for a suit for specific performance is generally three years: from the date fixed for performance, or, if no date is fixed, from when the plaintiff has notice that performance is refused. Identify which limb applies from the contract and correspondence. A demand notice does not stop or extend limitation by itself. Limitation must be calculated on the actual facts, including any legally material subsequent events.
  • Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, Order XXXIX (temporary injunctions) and Order VII (plaint contents).

VPossible steps, in sequence

  1. Read the agreement closely: time clauses, default clauses, whether the advance is part-payment or earnest money, and who bears title-defect risk.
  2. Check title and encumbrances, and whether the Seller has other co-owners or restrictions on transfer.
  3. Prepare proof that the Buyer has been, and is, ready and willing: funds, bank statements, loan sanction if any, written communications offering to complete.
  4. Send a written notice stating readiness to complete, requiring the Seller to perform, and recording the Buyer's position on the advance. Keep proof of service.
  5. If the Seller does not perform, file a suit for specific performance with alternative relief of refund and compensation. Consider whether and when the balance consideration must be deposited or secured; this depends on the court's directions and the specific facts.
  6. Apply for an interim order restraining the Seller from alienating the property or creating third-party rights, supported by the prima facie case, balance of convenience and risk of irreparable injury.
  7. Track the property records and registration office for any attempted sale; consider whether notice of the pending suit to prospective purchasers is advisable.

VIDocuments and evidence

Signed agreement (check stamping and registration); proof of every payment; identity and address proofs; Seller's title chain documents, revenue records and encumbrance certificate; correspondence including offers to complete; proof of Buyer's financial capacity; any possession-related documents; notice and proof of delivery.

VIIProceedings and forum

Civil court with territorial jurisdiction over the immovable property under the applicable CPC provisions, subject to the nature of the relief and statutory exceptions; pecuniary jurisdiction and court fee depend on the applicable valuation rules and State court-fee law. A routine private residential sale is not automatically a commercial dispute. Check local filing rules, valuation, stamp duty and any relevant State amendments before filing.

VIIIHow it could go

  • If the Buyer can show continuous readiness and willingness and the Seller's title is clear, a decree directing execution of the sale deed is possible.
  • If the Seller's title is defective or the third-party transferee proves good faith without notice, the practical relief may shift to refund with compensation.
  • If the Buyer cannot show readiness (for example, no funds available at the relevant dates), the claim for specific performance can fail even if the Seller was in breach.

No result is assured.

IXMisconceptions and lessons

  • "Paying an advance makes me the owner." Under the Transfer of Property Act framework an agreement to sell alone does not transfer ownership.
  • "I can wait and decide later." Delay and lack of continuous readiness can defeat specific performance.
  • "A higher offer lets the Seller cancel freely." The agreement's terms and the Specific Relief Act framework decide what the Seller can do.

Check stamping and registration at the start; diarise completion dates; keep evidence of funds; act promptly after any refusal or suspicious dealing.

XCommon questions

Can I get the property itself, or only my money back?

It depends on the terms of the agreement, the title, and whether a third party has acquired rights.

Does an unregistered agreement have any use?

It may still be relevant as evidence in some situations, subject to stamping and registration rules in the State concerned.

Does delay matter?

Yes. Both limitation and the buyer's readiness and willingness depend on dates.

XIWhat this scenario does not cover

Agricultural land with ceiling or tenure restrictions; builder-buyer disputes under RERA; co-owned or ancestral property; NRI sellers or buyers and FEMA.

XIIPrimary sources to consult

India Code: Specific Relief Act 1963 (as amended 2018); Transfer of Property Act 1882; Registration Act 1908; Limitation Act 1963; CPC 1908; the relevant State's stamp and court-fee statutes.
