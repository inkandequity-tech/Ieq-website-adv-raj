Service matters

Disputes between employees and public employers, from recruitment and promotion to disciplinary action and pension.

General information, last reviewed 13 October 2026.

What this covers

  • Recruitment and selection: eligibility, results, and decisions of selection bodies.
  • Promotion, seniority and pay fixation.
  • Suspension, departmental inquiries and punishment.
  • Termination, removal and reinstatement.
  • Pension, gratuity, family pension and other retirement benefits.
  • Transfers and compassionate appointment.

Where these matters are heard

The forum depends on the employer. Many central government employees go first to the Central Administrative Tribunal, which was set up under the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985. Other public employees usually approach the High Court under Article 226. Orders of a tribunal can be challenged in the High Court.

Before going to court, an employee is usually expected to use the remedies inside the department, such as an appeal or a representation.

Time limits to keep in mind

An application to the Central Administrative Tribunal generally has to be made within one year of the final order (Section 21 of the 1985 Act). The Tribunal can condone delay for sufficient cause. For a High Court writ, no fixed period applies, but delay can still count against the petitioner.

Papers that usually matter

  • The appointment letter and service book entries.
  • The order challenged, with proof of the date it was received.
  • For disciplinary matters: the charge sheet, the reply, the inquiry report and the punishment order.
  • For pension: the pension payment order and the service and salary records the department used.
  • Every representation sent and every reply.

Common questions

Can a recruitment result be challenged?

A candidate can approach the court where the selection rules were not followed or a decision was arbitrary. Courts generally look at the advertisement and the rules in force when the process began.

What is a departmental inquiry?

It is the process by which an employer examines a charge of misconduct. The employee is entitled to notice of the charges and a fair chance to reply, as the applicable conduct rules require and, for civil servants, as Article 311 requires where it applies.

Does a pending representation stop the time limit?

Not automatically. Where a representation has been made, the Tribunal's Act has its own rule on how time is counted. Anyone relying on a representation should have the dates checked early.
