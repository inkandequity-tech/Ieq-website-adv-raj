Pregnancy Cannot Bar a Woman from Public Employment: Inside the Allahabad HC's Komal Jaiswal Ruling

A Lucknow Bench sets aside UPSSSC's refusal to postpone a physical test for a nine-months-pregnant Forest Guard aspirant, holding that forcing a woman to choose between motherhood and a job violates both her right to reproduction and her right to livelihood.

IEQ Editorial Team Constitutional Law · Case Analysis 18 min read Special Appeal No. 318 of 2026
View Full Judgment (PDF) Download Judgment

On 22 July 2026, a Division Bench of the Allahabad High Court's Lucknow Bench, comprising Chief Justice Arun Bhansali and Justice Jaspreet Singh, delivered a ruling that has since travelled from courtroom to front page across Uttar Pradesh's biggest newspapers. In Komal Jaiswal v. State of U.P., the Court held that a woman's pregnancy cannot be treated as a ground to deny her the physical efficiency test (PET) required for a government job, and, more broadly, that denying such an accommodation forces a woman to choose between bearing a child and earning a livelihood, a choice the Constitution does not permit the State to impose.

Executive Summary

Komal Jaiswal, a candidate for the posts of Forest Guard and Wildlife Guard under a 2023 recruitment drive of the Uttar Pradesh Subordinate Services Selection Commission (UPSSSC), cleared her written examination in January 2026, more than two years after the original advertisement. By the time her physical efficiency test came up in February 2026, she was nine months pregnant. She asked the Commission to postpone her 14-kilometre walking test, to be completed within four hours, until after her delivery. The Commission refused, citing the absence of any express provision for postponement in the U.P. Forest Department Lower Subordinate (Forest Guard and Wildlife Guard) Service Rules, 2015. A learned Single Judge dismissed her writ petition without examining the merits of that refusal, relying instead on the fact that hundreds of other women candidates had already cleared their tests.

On appeal, the Division Bench reversed that outcome. It held that the Rules' silence on postponement was not a bar to relief, it was, if anything, a reason for the Commission to have exercised its discretion humanely. The Court set aside the Single Judge's order, allowed the writ petition, and directed UPSSSC to conduct Jaiswal's PET within four weeks, with a further direction that if she qualifies on merit, she will be appointed from the date on which a candidate lower in merit than her was appointed, and that one post in her category (OBC, women) be kept vacant until her result is declared.

Background: A Recruitment Drive Stretched Across Two Years

The case traces back to an advertisement issued on 12 September 2023 for 709 posts of Forest Guard and Wildlife Guard. Komal Jaiswal applied. In the ordinary course, a candidate's life does not pause because a recruitment process is slow, and hers didn't. She married in November 2023, shortly after applying, and conceived in May 2025. The written examination itself was not held until 9 November 2025, over two years after the advertisement, and her result, declaring her successful, was announced on 8 January 2026.

It was only after clearing the written exam that she was required to undergo the PET, scheduled for the window of 10–19 February 2026: a 14-kilometre walk to be completed on foot within four hours. By then she was in the final stage of pregnancy.

Timeline of the Case

  • November 2023, Jaiswal marries, during the pendency of the selection process.
  • May 2025, Jaiswal conceives.
  • 9 November 2025, Written examination conducted.
  • 8 January 2026, Jaiswal declared successful in the written exam.
  • 27 January 2026, Jaiswal, then in advanced pregnancy, writes to the Controller of Examination seeking postponement of her PET.
  • 10–19 February 2026, PET window scheduled; no response received to her request.
  • 13 February 2026, Jaiswal approaches the High Court before her scheduled PET date.
  • 18 February 2026, The High Court directs the Commission to file a short counter affidavit; the same day, the Commission's meeting formally rejects her postponement request, citing the absence of any enabling provision in the 2015 Rules.

  • 22 July 2026, The Division Bench allows Special Appeal No. 318 of 2026, sets aside the Single Judge's order, and directs UPSSSC to conduct Jaiswal's PET within four weeks.
  • 29–31 July 2026, The judgment is widely reported by the Times of India, Dainik Jagran and specialist legal portals across Uttar Pradesh and nationally.

The Litigation History

Jaiswal's path to relief was not a straight line. Her first writ petition (Writ-A No. 1833 of 2026) was dismissed by a Single Judge who did not, on the Court's own account, examine the substantive question she had raised, whether denying postponement to a nine-months-pregnant candidate was lawful. Instead, the dismissal turned on a numbers argument advanced by the Commission: that against 709 advertised posts, 332 women candidates had already cleared their medical examinations, and the final result was close to being forwarded to the government.

It was this dismissal that Jaiswal, represented by Advocate Rishabh Raj along with Advocate Radhika Singh, challenged in the special appeal before the Division Bench. Their central submission was that the two-year gap between advertisement and written examination was not of the candidate's making, that marriage and pregnancy in that interval were ordinary life events, and that the Single Judge had erred in treating the pace of other candidates' selection as a substitute for examining whether the denial of postponement was itself justified. The State and the Commission, represented by Anand Kumar Singh (Standing Counsel) and Saurabh Shankar Srivastava, maintained that the 2015 Rules simply did not provide for postponement, and that this absence of provision was sufficient ground for refusal.

The Issue Before the Court

The Division Bench framed the question with precision: where an "unforeseen inability like pregnancy or child birth" arises while a woman is in the process of seeking public employment, can her right to that employment be denied to her? At its core, this was not a dispute about the PET itself, or about physical fitness standards for forest and wildlife guards, it was a dispute about whether a recruiting authority may treat a rulebook's silence on an extraordinary circumstance as licence to refuse a reasonable accommodation.

The Court's Reasoning

The Bench began by establishing what was not in dispute: a married woman is not disqualified from appointment as a Forest Guard or Wildlife Guard, and pregnancy itself is not a bar to appointment under the 2015 Rules. Neither marriage nor pregnancy, in other words, disqualifies a candidate on the face of the Rules. The question was narrower, whether the Commission's discretion, when faced with a pregnant candidate unable to undertake a 14-kilometre walk within four hours, had to be exercised one way.

The Court held that it did. It reasoned that motherhood is "the most natural phenomenon in the life of a woman," and that where the recruitment process itself had stretched over two years, a delay attributable to the State, not the candidate, it was "expected of the Commission to be considerate and sympathetic" and to have recognised the physical difficulty a nine-months-pregnant woman would face in completing such a test. Jaiswal had asked only for a postponement of about a month, until after her delivery; she had not asked to be exempted from the test altogether. The Bench noted, pointedly, that even by the time of the appeal, no appointments against the 709 posts had actually been made, so a short postponement caused no prejudice to the recruitment timeline that the Commission could genuinely claim.

On the Rules' silence, the Court's reasoning is the crux of the judgment: the absence of an express provision for postponement is not the same as a prohibition on postponement. Where the Rules are silent and an extraordinary circumstance arises, the Commission retains the power, and, the Court suggested, the obligation, to exercise humane discretion. To read silence as a bar, the Bench held, would be to let a gap in subordinate legislation override a constitutional entitlement.

Most significantly, the Bench articulated the core constitutional harm in a single, widely quoted line: denial of postponement "essentially forces a woman to choose between bearing a child or employment, which cannot be permitted," because it interferes with both her right to reproduction and her right to employment. That formulation, treating the two rights as inseparable rather than competing, is what has made the judgment resonate well beyond the specific facts of one recruitment exercise.

On the Single Judge's reliance on the fact that 332 of 709 women candidates had already cleared their medical tests, the Division Bench was direct: that fact, by itself, could not justify denying Jaiswal her own chance to be tested. If she cleared her PET and medical examination and stood within the merit list for her category, appointment would follow in the ordinary course. The pace of other candidates' progress was irrelevant to whether she had been wrongly denied an opportunity.

The Operative Directions

Allowing the appeal, the Bench set aside the Single Judge's order of 6 July 2026 and allowed the original writ petition. It directed the respondents to:

  • Permit Jaiswal to undergo the PET pursuant to the 2023 advertisement, to be conducted and completed within four weeks (the Commission itself had indicated it would need this time to arrange a stadium and the test infrastructure);
  • Subject her to the remainder of the selection process, medical examination and merit assessment, in the ordinary course if she clears the PET;
  • If she stands in merit, appoint her with effect from the date on which a candidate lower in merit than her was appointed, in case appointments are made against the 709 posts before her result is declared; and
  • Keep one post in her category, OBC, women, vacant until her result is known, since the Commission had already made its recommendation to the State Government.

Constitutional Analysis

Although the judgment is compact, its constitutional register is unmistakable. Article 14 guarantees equality before the law; Article 15 prohibits discrimination on grounds including sex; Article 16 guarantees equality of opportunity in public employment; and Article 21's guarantee of life and personal liberty has, over decades of Indian constitutional jurisprudence, been read to encompass both the right to livelihood and the right to reproductive autonomy. The Bench's framing, that a woman cannot be made to choose between motherhood and employment, sits squarely within this lineage. It treats reproductive choice and the right to work not as separate, competing claims to be balanced against each other, but as two aspects of the same constitutional guarantee of dignity and equality that collapse into a single violation when the State forces a trade-off between them.

This approach echoes a broader arc in Indian service law, in which courts have progressively read protections for pregnant and maternal employees into constitutional guarantees rather than treating them as matters purely of statutory grace under the Maternity Benefit Act, 1961. The Bench's insistence that a rulebook's silence cannot be read as a bar on humane accommodation is, in that sense, a reaffirmation that subordinate legislation must yield to constitutional rights, not the other way around.

Employment Law Dimensions

From a service-law standpoint, the judgment is significant for what it says about administrative discretion in recruitment. Selection rules are, by necessity, drafted for the ordinary case; they cannot anticipate every extraordinary circumstance a candidate might face. The Commission's position, that because the 2015 Rules did not mention postponement, none could be granted, treated a gap in subordinate legislation as an absolute bar. The Division Bench rejected that logic, holding that the absence of a provision permitting an accommodation is not equivalent to a prohibition on it, and that recruiting authorities retain residual discretion to act reasonably and humanely in situations the Rules did not foresee.

The Court was also careful to add a proportionality check: it was not exempting Jaiswal from the PET, nor lowering the standard she would need to meet. It was only correcting the timing at which she would be tested, restoring her to the position she would have been in but for an accident of scheduling. That distinction matters for future cases: the judgment is not a ruling that pregnant candidates must be given a pass on physical standards, but that they cannot be denied a fair opportunity to meet those standards because of a temporary physical condition tied to pregnancy.

Women's Rights and Reproductive Autonomy

The newspapers that covered this judgment, from the Times of India's Uttar Pradesh edition to Dainik Jagran's Lucknow and Rajdhani pages, converged on the same headline framing: pregnancy is not a bar to public employment. That framing captures something the judgment makes explicit but that recruitment practice across many government bodies has, in effect, long denied: that the timing of pregnancy, marriage, or childbirth is not something a woman controls to fit a recruitment calendar, and a two-year gap between advertisement and examination, entirely the State's doing, should not be allowed to convert an ordinary life event into a disqualifying one.

The Bench's observation that the Commission ought to have been "more sympathetic and alive to the extraordinary situation" is a reminder that formal equality, treating every candidate identically regardless of circumstance, can itself produce discriminatory outcomes. Real equality, particularly for a right as gendered in its practical effect as pregnancy, sometimes requires accommodation rather than uniform treatment.

Media Coverage and Why the Case Resonated

The ruling was reported prominently on 30–31 July 2026. The Times of India's Lucknow edition ran the story under the headline "Why pregnant woman kept out of job test: HC," with the standfirst "Slams UPSSSC Decision," and carried a fuller account of the Bench's reasoning on an inside page, noting the Court's observation that authorities ought to have been "more sympathetic and alive to the extraordinary situation." A separate national edition of the paper carried a shorter item, "HC raps UPSSSC for denying PET to pregnant aspirant." Dainik Jagran's Lucknow edition covered it under "गर्भावस्था सरकारी नौकरी में बाधा नहीं: हाई कोर्ट" ("Pregnancy no bar to government job: High Court"), emphasising that the Division Bench had reversed the Single Judge's decision and had also rapped government departments more broadly for treating administrative delay as an excuse. Specialist legal portals, including LiveLaw, CourtBook, SupremeToday and LawBeat, carried detailed doctrinal write-ups of the judgment within days of it being uploaded.

Several things explain why a service-law special appeal, of the kind High Courts decide routinely, travelled this far. First, the fact pattern is instantly legible to a non-lawyer: a pregnant woman asked for a month's delay in a physical test and was refused. Second, the Bench's own language, "forces a woman to choose between bearing a child or employment", is quotable and captures a tension many working women recognise from their own experience, well beyond government recruitment. Third, the case arrived at a moment when recruitment delays in Uttar Pradesh's government hiring processes are themselves a matter of public frustration, and the judgment's observation that more than two years had elapsed between advertisement and written examination gave the ruling a second, more institutional edge: this was as much a comment on sluggish recruitment machinery as it was on any single Commission's inflexibility.

Why This Judgment Matters Beyond One Candidate

The direct beneficiary of this judgment is Komal Jaiswal, who will now get her postponed PET. But the reasoning has a wider reach. Government recruitment in India routinely takes years from advertisement to appointment, the gap here was over two years just to reach the written exam. Over such a timeframe, marriage, pregnancy, childbirth, and other life events are not aberrations; they are statistically near-certain for a portion of any large candidate pool. A recruitment framework that has no mechanism to accommodate these events, and that treats silence in its rules as a bar rather than an invitation to exercise discretion, will systematically and predictably disadvantage women candidates whose personal timelines happen to intersect with a slow-moving selection process.

By holding that rules need not expressly permit an accommodation for a recruiting body to grant one, the Court has effectively told every selection commission operating under similarly silent rules that the absence of a postponement clause is not a shield against a woman candidate's constitutional claim.

Impact on Future Recruitment Processes

Practically, recruiting authorities in Uttar Pradesh, and potentially beyond, given the persuasive value High Court rulings carry across jurisdictions, will need to reckon with several implications. Selection bodies conducting physical tests may find it prudent to build express postponement provisions into future recruitment rules for pregnancy, childbirth, and comparable medical circumstances, rather than leaving the question to be litigated case by case. Commissions may also need to reassess how they process representations from candidates facing genuine physical incapacity, given the Bench's clear signal that a request for a short, reasonable postponement should not be met with silence or blanket refusal grounded only in the Rules' silence. And recruiting bodies more broadly may face closer scrutiny of how long they take between advertisement and test administration, since the Court explicitly treated the two-year delay as relevant context for why the Commission's rigidity was unjustified.

Key Takeaways

  • Pregnancy and marital status cannot be treated as disqualifications for public employment, and cannot be used to deny a candidate a fair opportunity to complete a recruitment process.
  • Where selection rules are silent on postponement of a physical test, that silence does not bar a recruiting authority from granting one in extraordinary circumstances, silence is not prohibition.
  • Denying reasonable accommodation to a pregnant candidate forces an unconstitutional choice between motherhood and employment, implicating both reproductive rights and the right to livelihood.
  • The number of other candidates who have already cleared a stage of selection is not, by itself, a reason to deny one candidate a fair chance to be tested.
  • Recruiting authorities are expected to exercise sympathetic, humane discretion when recruitment timelines, often extended by the State's own delay, collide with a candidate's genuine physical incapacity.
